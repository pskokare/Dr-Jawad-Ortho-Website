What is joint replacement surgery?

Joint replacement surgery, also called arthroplasty, involves removing a damaged joint surface and replacing it with an artificial implant made of metal, ceramic or medical-grade plastic. The implant is shaped to move the way a healthy joint would, letting the patient bend, walk and bear weight with far less pain than before.

Cartilage inside a joint acts as a cushion between two bones. Once this cartilage wears away, usually from age-related arthritis, injury or long-standing inflammation, bone starts rubbing against bone. This is what produces the grinding pain and stiffness that eventually brings patients to an orthopedic clinic. In cases where the joint damage is limited, procedures such as arthroscopy may help delay the need for a full replacement, but once the cartilage is gone, arthroplasty remains the most reliable solution.

When do you need joint replacement?

Not every joint pain needs surgery. Doctors usually recommend joint replacement only after conservative treatment rest, physiotherapy, weight management, medication or injections has stopped giving relief. Options such as non-surgical spine and joint treatments are always explored first. Common signs that a joint has reached the stage where surgery may be appropriate include:

  • Pain that continues even while sitting or lying down
  • Stiffness that limits how far the joint can bend or straighten
  • A visible change in the shape of the knee or hip, such as bowing
  • Difficulty walking more than a short distance without stopping
  • Pain that disturbs sleep several nights a week
  • X-rays showing bone rubbing directly against bone

Types of joint replacement surgery we offer

Every joint behaves differently, and treatment is planned around which joint is affected and how much damage has occurred.

Total knee replacement

Total knee replacement resurfaces the entire knee joint and is usually recommended once arthritis affects most of the joint. It is the most commonly performed joint replacement procedure and delivers predictable, long-lasting pain relief.

Partial knee replacement

Partial knee replacement resurfaces only the damaged section of the knee, leaving healthy bone and ligaments untouched. It suits patients whose arthritis is limited to one compartment of the joint, and recovery is typically faster than after a total replacement.

Total hip replacement

Total hip replacement replaces the ball and socket of the hip joint and is commonly needed for advanced arthritis, avascular necrosis or a hip fracture that has damaged the joint beyond repair.

Shoulder replacement

Shoulder replacement addresses severe shoulder arthritis or complex fractures that have damaged the joint surface beyond repair. Both anatomic and reverse shoulder replacement options are available depending on the condition of the surrounding muscles.

Revision joint replacement

Revision joint replacement is a second surgery performed when an earlier knee or hip implant has worn out, loosened or become infected. It is a more complex procedure and requires a surgeon with specific revision experience.

What happens during the joint replacement journey

Every case starts with a clinical examination, a review of symptoms and imaging such as X-rays or an MRI. This confirms how much of the joint is damaged and whether surgery is the right next step or whether more conservative treatment should be tried first.

Once surgery is planned, patients go through routine blood tests, an anesthesia review and a discussion about the type of implant being used. Any existing health conditions such as diabetes or high blood pressure are brought under control before the date is finalized.

Most primary knee and hip replacements take between one and two hours. Patients are typically moved to a private room and encouraged to sit up or stand with support the same day.

Hospital stay after a joint replacement generally ranges from three to five days, depending on age, overall health and how quickly the patient regains mobility. Before discharge, the physiotherapy team reviews walking technique, stair climbing and home safety precautions.

Recovery and rehabilitation after joint replacement

Physiotherapy is not optional after joint replacement. It is what determines how much of the surgery's benefit a patient actually experiences. Most rehabilitation programs focus on regaining range of motion in the first two weeks, building strength over the following month, and returning to normal daily activity by around six to twelve weeks.

Long-term outcomes for joint replacement are well documented. A large population-based study published in The Lancet followed patients for up to twenty years and found implant survival rates above 95 percent at ten years for both knee and hip replacements, falling only gradually after that. In practical terms, most patients who undergo joint replacement surgery will not need a second surgery on the same joint for well over a decade, often for the rest of their life.

Frequently asked questions

How painful is joint replacement surgery?

Surgery itself is done under anesthesia, so patients feel nothing during the procedure. Pain after surgery is managed with a structured pain relief plan, and most patients describe the discomfort as manageable and steadily improving each day rather than severe.

What is the right age for joint replacement?

There is no fixed age cutoff. The decision depends on the extent of joint damage, pain levels and how much daily life is affected, not age alone. Both younger and older patients can be good candidates after a proper evaluation by a qualified joint replacement doctor in Hyderabad.

How long does a joint replacement implant last?

Modern implants are built to last. Long-term studies show survival rates above 95 percent at ten years and around 85 to 90 percent at twenty years for knee and hip replacements, meaning most patients need only one surgery in their lifetime.

Can both knees or both hips be replaced in the same surgery?

Yes, simultaneous bilateral replacement is possible in select, medically fit patients. This is decided after a detailed evaluation of overall health, heart and lung function, and personal preference.

Is robotic joint replacement better than conventional surgery?

Robotic joint replacement and computer-navigated systems improve the precision of implant positioning, which can support better alignment and potentially longer implant life.
